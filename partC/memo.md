# Decision Memo: Casual-Tone Replies in 6 Indian Languages

## Recommendation: Prompt-engineering only (Option C)

## Assumptions
- The main model already has reasonable fluency in all 6 languages
  (Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi) — the problem is
  *tone/register*, not basic language capability.
- "Casual" can be substantially achieved via few-shot examples and
  explicit style instructions, without changing model weights.
- Native review capacity (10h/week, Hindi+Kannada only) is the binding
  constraint on quality assurance, not compute.
- No external API budget means no GPT-4-as-judge or GPT-4-generated
  synthetic data at scale.

## Why not (a) SFT or (b) rewriter model
- SFT needs synthetic "casual" training pairs generated and vetted per
  language — with only Hindi+Kannada review coverage, 4 of 6 languages
  would ship un-reviewed training data, risking silently baking in bad
  or unnatural casual phrasing.
- A ≤1B rewriter model still needs training data + evaluation per
  language, and adds a second inference hop (latency/cost) — for a
  3-week timeline with one GPU, that's tight for 6 languages with
  limited native review.
- Prompt-engineering can be changed and re-reviewed in minutes, not
  days — critical given the compressed reviewer bandwidth.

## Back-of-envelope arithmetic
- **Reviewer throughput**: 10h/week x 3 weeks = 30 reviewer-hours,
  split across Hindi + Kannada only (~15h each). At ~2 minutes per
  reply reviewed, that's ~450 replies reviewed per language over the
  full window — enough to validate a prompt-engineering approach
  iteratively, not enough to validate a full SFT dataset (which
  typically needs thousands of examples reviewed for quality).
- **GPU usage**: near-zero for option C (only needed for generating a
  handful of few-shot examples, or optionally a *quick* validation
  run of a rewriter model as a stretch goal in week 3 if time allows).
  This leaves the full A100/2-week budget as slack/contingency,
  unlike (a) or (b), which would consume most or all of it directly.

## Success metric (numeric threshold)
Native-speaker reviewer rating on a 1-5 casualness scale: **>=70% of
sampled replies rated 4 or 5** for Hindi and Kannada (the only
languages with review coverage) after prompt tuning, versus baseline
formal-tone replies. For the 4 unreviewed languages, use a proxy: no
regression in fluency/coherence (existing automated checks), since
casualness itself can't be verified without native review.

## Kill criterion
If, by the **end of week 2**, Hindi/Kannada reviewer ratings have not
moved past **50% rated 4-5** despite 2+ rounds of prompt iteration,
abandon prompt-engineering-only and escalate to requesting a rewriter
model (option b) with the reserved GPU time, even though it compresses
the remaining timeline — because prompt-engineering alone can only be
as good as the base model's underlying stylistic range, and a plateau
this early signals hitting that ceiling.

## Day-1 experiment
Take 20 representative formal replies (already generated by the
current system) in Hindi and Kannada. Rewrite the *prompt* (not the
model) to include 3-5 few-shot casual-style examples plus an explicit
instruction ("respond the way a friend would text, not a textbook"),
regenerate the same 20 replies, and have the native reviewer rate
old-vs-new side by side on the 1-5 casualness scale that same day.
This gives an immediate, cheap signal on whether prompt-engineering
can move the needle at all before committing further reviewer/GPU time.
